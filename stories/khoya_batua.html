The Lost Purse

⏱ 4 min readहिन्दी में पढ़ें

Anu had a dream, and the dream had two wheels.

Every evening she watched the older children whiz down the village lane on their bicycles, bells ringing, dust flying behind them like a cape. One day, she told herself. One day I'll have a bicycle of my own. A red one, with a basket in front.

One morning, on her way past the market, Anu spotted something brown lying in the dust by the side of the road.

She bent down and picked it up. "Oh! It's somebody's purse!"

She opened it, just a little, and gasped. Inside was more money than she had ever held in her whole life. Folded notes, lots of them.

Anu closed her eyes, and right away she was flying. She was on a shiny red bicycle, racing down the lane, the wind in her hair, everyone turning to look. Ring, ring!

"With this money," she whispered, "I could get my bicycle..."

But then a small, quiet thought tapped her on the shoulder.

This purse isn't mine.

Anu opened her eyes. Somewhere, somebody had dropped this. Somebody was probably searching every corner right now, with a hollow, frightened feeling in their tummy. She knew that feeling. She'd had it the day she lost her school bag.

"Whoever lost it must be so worried," she said. And then, firmly, before she could change her mind: "I'll give it back."

She folded up the bicycle dream, tucked it away for another day, and walked back into the market.

There, beside his vegetable cart, stood old Ramu Kaka. He was turning out his pockets, lifting baskets of tomatoes, looking under sacks of onions. His face was grey with worry.

"Oh dear, oh dear," he kept saying. "Where has my purse gone? My grandson Munna's school fees were in it..."

Anu's heart thumped. "Kaka, why do you look so sad?"

"My dear, I've dropped my little brown purse somewhere, and..."

Anu held it out. "Is this your purse, Kaka?"

Ramu Kaka stared. Then his whole face lit up like a lamp. "Yes! Yes, my dear, that's it! That's my purse!" He held it to his chest. "Today you've saved my Munna's schooling."

"Please count it, Kaka," said Anu. "All the money is there."

Kaka pulled out a few rupees and pressed them toward her. "Here, take this as a reward."

Anu shook her head and pushed his hand gently back. "No, Kaka. Please keep it for Munna's fees."

Kaka laughed, a big warm laugh. "All right, all right! But then you simply must take this." And he picked the biggest, ripest, most golden mango from his cart and put it in her hands.

"Thank you, Kaka!" Anu smiled. "That's the sweetest reward of all."

That day Anu walked home with no bicycle and no money. Just a mango, sticky and sweet. And yet she felt so light that she almost skipped the whole way, as if she were riding the fastest bicycle in the world.

⭐ The lesson

Being honest keeps your heart light and happy.

💭 Think about it

Anu heard two voices in her head. When you hear two voices like that, how do you tell which one to listen to?
